The Global Fertility Decline And Its Contributing Factors

By Anoushka Arora, Omeed Zolali, and William Amorosi

Fertility Rates Have Fallen Across Much Of The World Since 1990

Around the world, birth rates are falling—and the reasons are more complex than a single cause. As education levels rise, economies shift, cities grow, and the costs of raising children increase, many people are choosing to have fewer children or to delay parenthood. This trend is shaped by a wide range of social, economic, cultural, and personal factors that influence how people think about family life. This visualization explores the many forces behind declining birth rates and how they vary across countries, regions, and generations.

Fertility Decline Appears Broad-Based Across Education Levels

Although it is true that countries with higher levels of tertiary education tend to have lower fertility rates, the relationship is not as straightforward as it may seem. When we look at fertility rates across different education levels within countries, we find that the decline in birth rates is occurring broadly across all education groups, not just among those with higher education. This suggests that while education may play a role in fertility decisions, it is not the sole or even the primary driver of the global fertility decline.

Family Policy Spending And Fertility

Public family spending varies widely across countries, both in total scale and in the mix of cash payments, services, and tax breaks. This view compares fertility rates against each spending measure as a share of GDP, making it easier to see whether higher spending is associated with higher fertility in a given year.

Although there is some variance, each region remains in their original "quadrant" of the graph. East Asia & Pacific experiences the largest shift from its original quadrant, but this is due to increasing internet penetration rates when they already had a low birth rate. Sub-Saharan Africa experiences a significant decrease in birthrates as their internet penetration rates rose from 2% to 33% in this period. Every region experienced some decrease in birth rates for its increase in internet penetration rates. The increase were not necessarily equal for for all regions (most had a major increase in internet penetration rates but a small decrease in birth rates). However, there is an observable trend. Put together, these factors suggest an association between internet penetration and a decrease in birth rates. Furthermore, they suggest that regional differences are not a significant reason for the decrease in birth rates in some areas. If there is a cultural reason for why some countries are not experiencing a birth rate decrease, it may be more valuable to explore those countries individually rather than broad regions that they are a part of.
